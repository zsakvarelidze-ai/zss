One list per level

L1 is continents and nothing else. L2 the continental regions under them. L3 the 198 countries and 43 territory sets. L4 and below are grouped continent → region → country, and a country's rows open on demand — the page carries the register with it, built from the same files the validator checks, and register.json is published beside it for anyone who wants the data alone.

Two numbers per row

The bold number is the row's place inside its parent — the last segment of its code. The faint one is its running number across the whole level, in register order. The code itself is the address and never changes when siblings are added.

How a territory is coded

A set sits in the region where the ground is, not where the holder is: Spain's is in North Africa, because the Canaries, Ceuta, Melilla and the plazas are. L3 carries one row per holder per region, numbered straight after that region's countries. Who holds a set is a field on the row, never part of the code, so a transfer of sovereignty changes no codes at all. Disputed and unclaimed ground has no holder and says so.

Retired, not removed

A code is an address: once issued it is never deleted, reused or renumbered, because somebody may have written it down. When a row turns out to name ground another code already names, it is superseded — struck through here, still listed, still resolving, pointing at the code that now holds the ground. It stops being counted, which is why a level's total can be one lower than the rows beneath it. Nothing is lost; the duplicate is.

One set, one tick

A set is one thing made of pieces that do not touch, so its L3 checkbox takes the whole set at once. L4 is where the pieces are named and each carries its own box — the Canaries alone, the plazas alone. Drop one and the set's box goes half-filled, which is the register saying you are holding part of a set, not all of it.

Where L6 came from

The L6 sweep of 16–17 September placed each geoBoundaries ADM2 unit inside its ADM1 parent by point-in-polygon on the source geometry and matched that parent to an existing L5 row by name. Countries whose names did not all resolve were left out rather than guessed; 44 more wait on the share-alike ruling.

What a level heading means

“Provinces”, “Districts” and the rest are glosses, not definitions. Below L3 a level is depth in that country’s own ladder: Rwanda’s provinces sit at L4 and its districts at L5, while France’s régions sit at L4 and its départements at L5. Both are right. What holds everywhere is containment — a bigger division above a smaller one, each wholly holding its children, nothing overlapping and nothing spanning two parents. Nothing is lost by this: L5 never was a province tier — Cabo Verde’s is islands, Ireland’s counties, the UK’s its own grouping — and the kind column on each row carries the country’s own word, filled on 97% of them. Depth is the level’s job; what a thing is, is kind’s.

What a row is

The level says how deep; the word after the name says what. Kenya's L5 are counties, Rwanda's districts. New L6 rows carry no word yet because the source does not say and the register does not assert.

How deep it goes

Depth follows the ground. Georgia runs to L8, and so now do Bangladesh, South Africa and Sierra Leone; 21 countries reach L7; most stop at L6; L9 — settlements — is empty until the gazetteer layer is allowed through. A level left empty for a place means nothing has been entered below it, which is not yet the same as nothing being there.
